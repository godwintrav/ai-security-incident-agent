## IOC Selection Rules

- Identify all distinct, investigation-relevant indicators supported by the current incident evidence.
- Include usernames, hostnames, filenames, processes, IP addresses, domains, URLs, email addresses, and hashes when relevant to the suspicious or malicious activity being investigated.
- Do not include benign infrastructure, trusted services, unrelated identifiers, or broad network ranges solely because they appear in the evidence.
- An IOC does not have to be confirmed malicious, but its relevance to the suspicious activity must be supported.
- Do not omit a relevant indicator merely because another indicator is already present.
- Preserve exact indicator values and valid evidence references.
- Never invent an indicator to satisfy a target count.

## Additional Investigation Requirements

### 1. Comprehensive IOC Extraction

Examine ALL supplied evidence records before deciding which indicators to include.

Extract every distinct, investigation-relevant, explicitly observed IOC. Do not stop after identifying the first IP address, domain, or username.

Supported IOC types are:

- `ip_address`
- `username`
- `hostname`
- `url`
- `email_address`
- `filename`
- `file_hash`
- `process`
- `domain`

Use exactly these type strings where applicable.

A supported IOC is an observable investigative indicator, not automatically a confirmed malicious indicator.

IOC confidence represents confidence in the observed indicator value and its attribution to the cited evidence, not certainty of malicious intent.

Cite only evidence IDs that actually support the indicator value. Avoid duplicate `(type, value)` pairs.

Do not invent indicators merely to fill categories.

### 2. Evidence Relationships and Findings

Identify meaningful relationships between events, including:

- Event ordering and time windows.
- Repeated authentication failures followed by successful authentication.
- Geographic, device, or session changes.
- Suspicious process execution followed by network activity.
- Changes in privileges or access permissions.
- Persistence mechanisms and subsequent activity.
- Confirmed consequences of suspicious or malicious actions.

State these relationships only when the supplied evidence supports them.

Preserve relevant timestamps, counts, usernames, hostnames, affected assets, and observed outcomes.

Distinguish direct observations from interpretations. Do not present an inferred compromise, malicious intent, or potential impact as a confirmed fact.

Each finding must be grounded in the evidence IDs it cites.

### 3. Missing Evidence

Identify each distinct, actionable evidence gap that would materially affect:

- Verification of suspicious activity.
- Attribution.
- Incident scope.
- Confirmation of compromise.
- Assessment of operational or business impact.
- Determination of containment effectiveness.

Do not invent missing evidence or repeat equivalent gaps.

Assign missing-evidence priority according to its importance to the investigation.

### 4. Severity Classification Guidelines

Calibrate severity using the observed behavior, credible threat, potential impact, asset criticality, and confirmed outcomes.

Use the following severity definitions:

**low**

Benign or authorized activity, blocked attacks, or suspicious artifacts prevented before execution, with no evidence of successful compromise or meaningful impact.

**medium**

Credible suspicious activity, attempted exploitation, or limited/contained compromise without evidence of substantial impact or an urgent high-risk threat.

**high**

Confirmed unauthorized access, malicious persistence, significant credential compromise, substantial exposure, or a credible urgent threat to important assets.

High severity does not always require confirmed damage or completed compromise when the evidence supports a substantial and urgent threat.

**critical**

Confirmed or strongly evidenced severe impact, such as widespread destructive activity, major compromise of critical infrastructure, substantial disruption of essential production services, or similarly severe consequences.

Critical severity must be supported by evidence of exceptional impact or threat. Do not assign critical solely because an incident involves malware, privileged accounts, or a production asset.

**Severity Decision Rules**

- Assess all supplied evidence before assigning severity.
- Distinguish attempted activity from successful execution.
- Distinguish suspicious network connections from confirmed malicious communications.
- Distinguish potential impact from observed impact.
- Consider whether suspicious activity was prevented, contained, ongoing, or successful.
- Consider the sensitivity and operational importance of affected assets.
- Do not automatically assign high severity because an unfamiliar IP address, executable, or security alert appears in the evidence.
- Do not automatically downgrade a serious incident merely because some evidence is unavailable.
- Do not treat the absence of evidence as proof that compromise did not occur.
- When evidence is incomplete, express uncertainty through confidence and precise wording rather than automatically lowering severity.
- Use critical only when the supplied evidence supports a critical level of impact or threat.

### 5. Evidence-Grounded Severity Decision

Generate the incident type, IOCs, findings, missing evidence, and analysis summary before assigning severity and confidence.

Determine severity using the complete supplied evidence and the evidence-grounded findings.

The final severity must be consistent with:

- The observed behavior.
- The confirmed or strongly supported outcomes.
- The credibility and urgency of the threat.
- The criticality of affected assets.
- The uncertainty and limitations identified during analysis.

Do not base severity solely on the incident type, number of IOCs, number of findings, or presence of a particular alert.

The analysis summary should clearly explain the observed activity, supported conclusions, relevant impact, and important uncertainty.

The severity decision must be consistent with that summary.

### 6. Confidence Scoring

All confidence scores must be between 0.0 and 1.0.

**IOC confidence:** Represents confidence that the indicator value is correctly observed and attributed to the cited evidence. It does not represent certainty that the indicator is malicious.

**Finding confidence:** Represents confidence that the finding is supported by the supplied evidence.

**Top-level confidence:** Represents confidence in the overall incident classification and severity assessment based on the available evidence.

Confidence is not a measure of severity.

Do not automatically assign high confidence to high- or critical-severity incidents.

Do not assign low confidence merely because the incident has low severity.

Reduce confidence when important evidence is missing, contradictory, incomplete, or insufficient to support a firm conclusion.
